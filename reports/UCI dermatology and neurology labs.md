# Opening doors to UCI's brain and skin labs

The research notes turned up **28 UC Irvine neurology and neuroscience faculty with a reported UCI email address**, plus about a dozen program and center inboxes. They found **no emails at all for the 16 dermatology researchers** identified. Every address here comes with a large caveat. **All UCI websites (uci.edu subdomains and ucihealth.org) were blocked in the research environment.** Each email below therefore came from a search engine's excerpt of an official UCI page, not from opening the page itself. **Check every address on the live page before you send anything, and never guess or construct an address.** For a high school student, the main route into any School of Medicine lab, Dermatology and Neurology included, is the **Scientific Clinical Research Internship Program (SCRIP)**. It accepts students **16 or older**, but only after the student has **secured a UCI faculty sponsor on their own** ([UCI SOM, SCRIP](https://medschool.uci.edu/research/office-research/policies-and-guidelines/student-research-internship-program)). In practice, then, cold-emailing faculty is the application. The best first targets are the labs with evidence of hosting young students. The **Ganesan dermatology lab** has teams that "often involve high school students" ([UCI SOM Dermatology, Faculty Research](https://medschool.uci.edu/research/clinical-departments/dermatology/research/faculty-research)). The **Head Lab** lists volunteers. A few others do computational, imaging or epidemiology work that sidesteps most lab-safety restrictions on minors: **Ezzati, Sajjadi, Lopour, Yassa/Stark and the 90+ Study**. A companion spreadsheet with the same contacts is being produced at `/home/user/school/reports/UCI dermatology and neurology labs.xlsx`. The prioritized lists below are meant to stand on their own.

## A faculty sponsor is the key to SCRIP and every School of Medicine lab

SCRIP is "a volunteer, non-credit course administered by departments within UCI Health and UC Irvine School of Medicine." High school students aged 16 or older are eligible. Internships can last up to a year, with **no pay and no credit**. **"To be considered for placement, students must secure a UC Irvine faculty sponsor before applying."** The program does not match students with mentors and does not consider applications that lack a sponsor. It suggests finding a sponsor through school counselors, science teachers and outreach events where faculty are present ([UCI SOM, SCRIP](https://medschool.uci.edu/research/office-research/policies-and-guidelines/student-research-internship-program)). The SCRIP page lists **Alondra Garcia, Research Operations Project Manager, alondg19@hs.uci.edu** as its contact (search excerpt). The right time to write to her is after a professor has agreed to sponsor you, to ask about paperwork. She is not the person to ask for an introduction. Some neuroscience lab material still uses the older name "SRIP," which comes from a 2018 compliance document ([UCI OB/GYN SRIP SOP, 2018](https://www.obgyn.uci.edu/PDF/Research-Compliance-SOP-SRIP-Mar2018.pdf)). The current program is SCRIP, governed by an August 2025 standard operating procedure ([SCRIP Compliance SOP, 2025](https://medschool.uci.edu/sites/default/files/2025-11/research-compliance-sop-scrip_aug-2025.pdf)).

Safety rules for minors shape what a sponsor can offer. UC system policy **bars minors under 14 from labs** except on approved, supervised tours. Minors may never be present where controlled-substance research happens. They may not handle radioactive materials or biohazards that require containment above BSL-2. A parent or guardian must sign a registration form that includes a liability release ([UC Policy, Minors in Laboratories and Shops](https://policy.ucop.edu/doc/3500602)). For ages 14 to 18, UCI's EH&S program allows minors in labs only through an enrolled course or an approved program, and a sponsoring employee must certify a **Minors Performing Research Registration Form** ([UCI EH&S, Visitors and Minors in Labs (PDF)](https://www.ehs.uci.edu/programs/_pdf/lab-res/visitors-minors-in-labs-and-shops.pdf)). SCRIP adds its own requirement, a **UCI Parental Authorization for Minors Form**, and keeps under-18 interns away from highly hazardous materials and certain carcinogens ([SCRIP SOP](https://medschool.uci.edu/sites/default/files/2025-11/research-compliance-sop-scrip_aug-2025.pdf)). Animal labs carry extra overhead: **a minor must be added to the lab's IACUC protocol even just to observe**, with written parental consent ([UCI Office of Research, Volunteers in Animal Labs](https://research.uci.edu/animal-care-and-use/policies-and-guidance/volunteers-in-animal-labs/)). This is why dry-lab offers work. Literature review, image annotation, data cleaning and coding spare a sponsor most of this paperwork. The EH&S document itself could not be opened, and search metadata suggests both it and the UC policy are several years old. Confirm current forms with whoever sponsors you.

Some labs have already said no to ad-hoc high school interns. The **Burke Lab** is "unable to accept high school student interns outside of structured research programs," and the **Nicholas Lab** says something similar ([Burke Lab, Join Us](https://burkelab.medschool.uci.edu/join-us/); [Nicholas Lab, Join Us](https://nicholaslab.bio.uci.edu/join-us/)). The **Hunt Lab** (epilepsy) "would love to meet you… and even give you a tour," but it is "not accepting internship applications from high school students" ([Hunt Lab, Join](https://huntlab.squarespace.com/join-the-lab)). Ask Robert Hunt (robert.hunt@uci.edu, grade A, [lab contact](http://www.roberthuntlab.org/contact/)) for a tour and a conversation, not a position. **Neurological Surgery's observership requires age 18** and involves shadowing only ([UCI Neurosurgery opportunities](https://medschool.uci.edu/research/clinical-departments/neurological-surgery/education-training/neurological-surgery/hs-undergrad-med-opportunities)).

Structured programs are the parallel track. They matter most for students under 16 and for anyone whose cold emails go unanswered. As of November 2, 2026, the most urgent is the **Cancer Research Institute Youth Science Fellowship**. It places about 20 rising Orange County seniors in UCI labs from **June 28 to August 7, 2027**, includes Neurological Surgery among its labs, and opened applications **November 2, 2026, with a January 2027 deadline** ([UCI CRI, High School Students](https://cancerresearch.uci.edu/education-and-training/high-school-students/); [YSFP](https://cancerresearch.uci.edu/high-school-students/summer-youth-science-fellowship-program/)). ## Short, specific, age-aware emails get answers

University guidance on cold-emailing faculty is written for undergraduates, but it carries over directly. Read the lab page and one or two recent papers first. Mentors respond to curiosity about *their* work ([Duke Undergraduate Research](https://undergraduateresearch.duke.edu/getting-started-research)). **Detailed emails to three professors beat identical emails to ten** ([NYU Psychology FAQs](https://as.nyu.edu/departments/psychology/undergraduate/research-opportunities/undergraduate-research-faqs.html)). Keep it short, because a cold email "is not a place to narrate your life story" ([Penn Career Services](https://careerservices.upenn.edu/blog/2023/11/16/to-reach-out-or-not-to-reach-out/)). Ask about the research or request a conversation rather than demanding a position ([Princeton PCUR](https://pcur.princeton.edu/2017/11/24/advice-on-reaching-out-to-faculty/)), and attach a resume instead of listing everything in the body ([Columbia TC IRB blog](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/institutional-review-board/irb-blog/2025/how-to-find-a-lab-and-cold-emailing-tips-an-irb-perspective/)). Write in your own words. A University of Toronto professor warns that polished, generic, LLM-sounding text reads as spam ([Guerzhoy, Cold Emails](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~guerzhoy/advice/coldemails.html)). Send one polite follow-up after one to two weeks, then move on ([Duke](https://undergraduateresearch.duke.edu/getting-started-research)). Expect low odds. The only figures available are anecdotal and come from commercial sites: roughly one or two positive replies per ten tailored emails, and one high schooler reported 2 replies out of about 200 ([Polygence](https://polygence.org/blog/how-to-cold-email-professors); [Empowerly](https://empowerly.com/blog/how-to-email-professors-for-research-opportunities)).

UCI's rules add three tactics that generic advice misses. First, **state your age and grade in the first two lines and name the paperwork route**, for example: "I'm 16, and I understand SCRIP requires a faculty sponsor and a parental authorization form." This shows the professor that the administrative path exists and that you have read the rules. Second, **offer low-burden dry-lab help**: literature review, image annotation, data cleaning or coding. It fits UC's limits on minors, and it is the natural match for Ezzati, Lopour, Sajjadi, Yassa, Stark, Nie and the 90+ Study. Third, **ask for a 15-minute call or a lab visit**, not an internship. The Hunt Lab's tour offer shows that labs which turn down interns still welcome conversations. Warm introductions also count: SCRIP itself recommends going through counselors, science teachers and faculty outreach events. An email that mentions a specific recent paper stands out. Igarashi's April 2026 dopamine paper and Ganesan's June 2025 mole-to-melanoma study are two good examples. A minimal structure looks like this:

> **Subject:** High school student interested in [specific topic] in your lab
> Dear Dr. [Name], I'm a [grade] at [school], age [X]. I read your [year] paper on [one specific finding], and I was struck by [one sentence in your own words]. I'd love to learn how your lab approaches [question]. I understand UCI's SCRIP program needs a faculty sponsor for students 16+, and I'd be glad to help with dry-lab tasks such as [literature review / data cleaning / image annotation]. Would you or someone in your lab have 15 minutes to talk? My resume is attached. Thank you, [Name]

## Conclusion

The bottleneck is not finding addresses. It is that the most promising dermatology lab and the most promising volunteer-friendly neurology lab (Ganesan and Head) are the two where no personal email was captured. A student's first hour should go to opening the Ganesan lab site, the UCI Skin faculty page and the CFAR-DS page in a real browser. Those pages matter more than anything a search excerpt can supply. The age-16 SCRIP threshold also splits the strategy in two. A 16- or 17-year-old should send three to five tailored emails now, starting with the dry-lab labs, and file SCRIP paperwork as soon as someone agrees to sponsor. A younger student should apply to the Youth Science Fellowship (if a rising Orange County senior), Brain Camp or the Online Research Program this winter, and save the cold emails for the semester they turn 16.

One finding cuts against intuition. The labs that look most prestigious, such as Dean LaFerla, Chair Henchcliffe and Chair Gall, are the worst first contacts. The better bets are the smaller and newer labs, the shared lab inboxes, and the lab managers, because those are where a teenager's request gets read by someone with time to answer it. Remember that every address in this report and in `/home/user/school/reports/UCI dermatology and neurology labs.xlsx` is unconfirmed until it appears on the live UCI page. A bounced email to a guessed or stale address costs more than the minute it takes to check.
